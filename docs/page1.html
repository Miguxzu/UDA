Electronic Waste: A Growing Global Issue

Every year, the world produces millions of tons of electronic waste, or e-waste. But what exactly is e-waste, and why is it important to manage it responsibly?

Find Out More

Definition of E-Waste

🗑️
What is e-waste?
E-waste is all those electrical or electronic devices that are no longer wanted or used. Not just old computers and smartphones, but also fridges, washing machines, microwaves, and even toys with electronic parts. In 2019, the world produced 53.6 million metric tons of e-waste—and the amount keeps growing every year.
📈
Why is e-waste growing?
Technology changes fast, products don’t last as long as they used to, and we buy new devices more often. That’s why e-waste is the fastest-growing type of waste in the world.

What Items Become E-Waste?

E-waste includes computers, laptops, tablets, mobile phones, TVs, printers, monitors, kitchen appliances, and even toys with electronic parts. Basically, any device with a plug, battery, or circuit board can become e-waste.

Why Is E-Waste a Problem?

Many electronic devices contain hazardous substances such as lead, mercury, cadmium, and flame retardants. If not properly managed, these substances can pollute soil, water, and air, posing risks to human health and the environment.

Recovering Valuable Materials

E-waste is also a source of valuable materials like gold, silver, copper, and rare earth elements. Proper recycling allows us to recover these resources, reducing the need for new mining and lowering environmental impact.

The Impact of E-Waste

♻️
What happens to e-waste?
Only about 17% of e-waste is officially collected and recycled worldwide (UN). The rest often ends up in landfills or is handled informally, especially in poorer countries, where unsafe recycling can harm both people and the environment.
⚠️
Why is it dangerous?
When e-waste is dumped in landfills, toxic substances can leak into the soil and water. Informal recycling—like burning cables to get copper—releases dangerous chemicals into the air, putting workers and communities at risk.
